# Why People Click the Thing They Hate  
## An affect and audience research memo on clickbait, junk content, AI slop, and low-quality attention media

## Abstract

Low-quality attention media works because it does not need to satisfy audiences in the ordinary sense. It only needs to interrupt, arouse, promise resolution, and produce a monetizable action before disappointment sets in. Clickbait, junk news, MFA pages, SEO spam, AI slop, and disinformation-for-profit are different formations, but they share a conversion logic: affect is translated into clicks, dwell time, shares, ad impressions, affiliate redirects, training data, and platform signals. Audiences are not simply “fooled.” They often recognize manipulation, resent it, and continue clicking because curiosity, anger, fear, hope, recognition, habit, social belonging, and algorithmic conditioning operate faster than reflective judgment.

Current evidence complicates the simple “bad headlines trick gullible people” account. Curiosity-gap headlines can backfire, and clickbait can reduce trust and satisfaction; nevertheless, even low-satisfaction clicks can be profitable in ad markets and recommendation systems. Research on moral outrage, misinformation sharing, and platform reward structures suggests that people share and engage not only because they believe content, but because engagement performs identity, manages uncertainty, signals belonging, or follows learned habits. AI slop intensifies these dynamics by lowering production costs, increasing volume, blurring authenticity cues, and creating a generalized suspicion that can damage even legitimate media.

## Core thesis

**Low-quality attention media succeeds by separating the economics of attention from the psychology of satisfaction.** The audience’s felt experience may be annoyance, disgust, cynicism, or disappointment, but the infrastructure counts a click, view, comment, scroll, share, or ad auction as success. The result is a media economy where negative affect, distrust, and fatigue are not failures of the system; they are often usable inputs.

This does not mean every actor in the system is malicious. Many writers, editors, translators, annotators, freelancers, creators, and low-wage contractors are operating under precarious conditions and algorithmic management. Google’s site-reputation-abuse policy debate, for example, has affected publishers and freelancers as media companies respond to search penalties and revenue pressures. ([developers.google.com](https://developers.google.com/search/blog/2024/11/site-reputation-abuse?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) The affective economy should therefore be analyzed as a labor-platform-advertising system, not only as a morality play about “bad content.”

## Terminology: related but not identical systems

| Term | Primary unit | Core mechanism | Audience experience | Revenue/incentive logic |
|---|---:|---|---|---|
| **Clickbait** | Headline, thumbnail, teaser, notification | Curiosity gap, emotional arousal, ambiguity, withheld resolution | “I know this is manipulative, but I want closure” | Click-through, traffic spikes, social sharing |
| **Content farm** | Publishing organization or network | High-volume, templated, low-cost production targeted to searchable or shareable demand | Shallow usefulness, repetitive style, search-answer veneer | Search traffic, display ads, affiliate links |
| **Content mill** | Labor arrangement | Piece-rate or low-paid writing/editing/translation optimized for speed and volume | Often invisible to audience; visible as generic prose | Labor arbitrage; cheap content supply |
| **MFA site** | Ad-arbitrage website | Buys/attracts traffic, surrounds thin content with high ad density and auctions | Clutter, pop-ups, slow pages, accidental clicks | Programmatic ad revenue; impression maximization |
| **SEO spam** | Search manipulation tactic | Scaled pages, parasite SEO, expired domains, hidden links, scraped or templated content | Search results feel polluted; user distrust | Ranking capture, lead generation, affiliate capture |
| **Junk news** | News-like domain/post/page | Low professional standards, conspiratorial or hyperpartisan framing, deceptive style | “Feels like news,” but unstable credibility | Ads, political influence, donations, list-building |
| **AI slop** | Synthetic low-quality media | Mass AI generation, remixing, hallucination, scraped templates, cheap visual/audio mimicry | Uncanny, repetitive, frictionless, sometimes hard to identify | Scale advantage; low production cost; algorithm gaming |
| **Disinformation-for-profit** | Campaign or publishing system | False or misleading content optimized for attention and monetization | Anger, fear, identity affirmation, suspicion | Attention arbitrage, ads, fundraising, scams |
| **Platform gaming** | General incentive response | Reverse-engineering ranking/recommendation/monetization systems | “The algorithm wants this” | Visibility, revenue, status, political reach |

Google’s current Search spam policies explicitly target attempts to deceive users or manipulate Search systems, including manipulations of generative AI responses in Search; its policy list includes scaled content abuse, site reputation abuse, expired domain abuse, cloaking, link abuse, and scam/fraud patterns. ([developers.google.com](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) In advertising, ANA benchmark work shows why MFA matters economically: programmatic budgets still contain major inefficiencies even as MFA reduction improves, with Q1 2025 participants directing 41% of budgets to “effective ad impressions” and a large optimization gap remaining. ([s3.amazonaws.com](https://s3.amazonaws.com/media.mediapost.com/uploads/ANAQ12025Programmatic.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

## 1. The affective conversion funnel

The audience-side funnel often looks like this:

**Interrupt → arouse → withhold → resolve poorly → monetize anyway → retarget/recommend → habituate.**

A user sees a headline, thumbnail, push notification, search snippet, short video, or influencer clip. The item does not need to be good; it needs to create a small emotional or cognitive imbalance. Curiosity-gap work explains why a teaser that withholds a key detail can create a perceived “information gap,” but recent registered-report evidence shows such gaps do not always outperform concrete headlines and may backfire depending on context. ([nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-81575-9?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) That is important: clickbait is not magic. It is a probabilistic tactic embedded in platforms that can test many variants cheaply.

Even when the content disappoints, the market may already have counted success. A click may trigger ads, cookie syncs, analytics events, affiliate redirects, newsletter pop-ups, remarketing segments, and ranking signals. Croatian survey research on clickbait found emphasis on disappointment, dissatisfaction, and credibility damage, which supports the idea that clickbait can be economically rational while socially corrosive. ([mlar.sk](https://www.mlar.sk/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/5_Igor-Kaniz%E2%95%A0iaj-Boris-Beck_Stela-Lechpammer_Igor-Weidlich-.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

## 2. Psychological and affective mechanisms

| Mechanism | How it works | Why it helps low-quality media | Typical content forms |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Curiosity gap** | Withholds a salient answer; creates discomfort until resolved | User clicks to close the gap even when suspicious | “You won’t believe…,” blurred thumbnails, listicles |
| **Surprise** | Violates expectation; invites checking | Makes thin content feel novel | Weird facts, bizarre AI images, fake “breaking” clips |
| **Outrage / moral emotion** | Converts judgment into action; “someone must see this” | Comments, shares, quote-posts, dunking all count | Political bait, culture-war posts |
| **Fear** | Threat monitoring; need for control | Encourages doomscrolling, alerts, repeated checking | Crime, disease, scams, collapse narratives |
| **Disgust** | Boundary defense; contamination response | Produces compulsive avoidance/inspection loop | Gross thumbnails, moral disgust, “look how awful” posts |
| **Hope** | Promise of rescue, cure, wealth, revelation | Powers scams, miracle cures, finance bait | “One simple trick,” supplements, crypto |
| **Nostalgia** | Restores continuity and identity | Makes low-effort recycling feel emotionally rich | AI “old days” videos, retro listicles |
| **Parasocial attachment** | Trust shifts from institution to familiar persona | Influencers can launder weak claims through intimacy | Podcasts, livestreams, creator news |
| **Variable reward** | Intermittent payoff keeps behavior going | Most items disappoint, but some amuse or help | Infinite scroll, Shorts, TikTok, feeds |
| **Cognitive load** | Fatigue reduces scrutiny | Users satisfice: click first, evaluate later | Search spam, dense ad pages, “explainer” slop |
| **Media fatigue** | Overload creates resignation | Cynical users still consume what is easiest | Aggregators, repetitive outrage cycles |
| **Distrust** | Suspicion becomes engagement rather than exit | Users click to debunk, mock, verify, or rage-share | “Can this be real?” posts |
| **Learned helplessness** | Users feel the system is polluted anyway | Reduces expectation of quality; lowers resistance | Search pollution, AI slop feeds |

The strongest general pattern is not simply “negative emotions win.” Research on virality shows high-arousal emotions drive sharing: awe, anger, and anxiety tend to travel better than low-arousal sadness. ([jonahberger.com](https://jonahberger.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/02/ViralityB.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) Moral outrage is especially platform-compatible: a 2024 Science study found misinformation exploits outrage to spread, while earlier work found likes and shares can reinforce future outrage expression through social learning. ([science.org](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.adl2829?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

## 3. Why people click content they know is bad

People click “bad” content for at least eight different reasons:

1. **Closure:** The headline creates an unfinished mental object. The click is a cheap way to end the itch.
2. **Verification:** The user distrusts the claim but clicks to see whether it is false, exaggerated, funny, or enraging.
3. **Social preparation:** The user wants to know what others are reacting to.
4. **Identity performance:** Sharing, mocking, or condemning content signals group membership.
5. **Habit:** Platform rewards train repeated engagement faster than reflective accuracy checks.
6. **Low stakes:** The user thinks, “It is only a click.”
7. **Background fatigue:** When everything feels low quality, discrimination weakens.
8. **Occasional payoff:** Sometimes the bad-looking thing is useful, funny, emotionally satisfying, or socially valuable.

The “habit” point is important. Ceylan, Anderson, and Wood argue that misinformation sharing is not only laziness or partisan bias; platform reward structures can produce habitual sharing patterns, and incentives can also build more truth-sensitive habits. ([pnas.org](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2216614120?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) This helps explain why people share things they distrust: the act of sharing is often not a full epistemic endorsement. It can be a joke, warning, identity badge, outrage performance, social probe, or habitual feed action.

## 4. What emotions are converted into clicks?

Low-quality attention media is an affect refinery. It does not merely “make people emotional”; it converts particular affects into measurable actions.

| Emotion/affect | Conversion action | Why it monetizes | Failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|
| Curiosity | Click/open | Ad load, pageview, dwell | Disappointment, credibility loss |
| Anger | Comment/share/quote-post | Engagement ranking, virality | Polarization, harassment |
| Fear | Refresh/subscribe/follow | Repeat visits, alerts, scams | Anxiety, doomscrolling |
| Disgust | Watch/inspect/share | Strong attention lock | Desensitization, moral panic |
| Hope | Click affiliate/buy/sign up | High conversion value | Fraud, exploitation |
| Nostalgia | Watch longer, share | Identity-safe engagement | Reactionary mythmaking |
| Parasocial warmth | Trust recommendation | Sponsorship/affiliate power | Manipulation, hidden ads |
| Cynicism | Hate-click, mock-share | “Negative engagement” still counts | Platform nihilism |
| Fatigue | Passive scrolling | More impressions | News avoidance |

Doomscrolling sits at the intersection of fear, control-seeking, and variable reward. Recent work links doomscrolling to anxiety, distrust, suspicion, despair, and vicarious trauma, while HCI studies of infinite scroll show that platform design can extend time-on-platform even when users later report regret or reduced satisfaction. ([theguardian.com](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/article/2024/jul/19/doomscrolling-linked-to-existential-anxiety-distrust-suspicion-and-despair-study-finds?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

## 5. How clickbait can produce dissatisfaction and still profit

Clickbait does not require durable trust if the revenue model rewards short-term extraction. A page can disappoint the reader and still:

- register a pageview;
-- trigger multiple ad auctions;
- produce viewability metrics;
- collect behavioral data;
- redirect to an affiliate offer;
- increase platform engagement;
- teach the algorithm that similar teasers generate response.

This is the central mismatch: **user satisfaction is a slow, reputational metric; monetizable engagement is immediate.** Clickbait research on Facebook found that clickbait affects likes, shares, and comments, but platform effects vary by topic and format. ([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9242456/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) Meanwhile, work on headline concreteness suggests that curiosity-gap success is conditional rather than universal. ([nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-81575-9?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) So the system does not need one perfect manipulation; it needs high-volume testing, cheap production, and enough positive outliers.

## 6. AI slop and the trust problem

AI slop changes the audience problem in four ways.

First, it lowers the cost of producing plausible-but-empty content. NewsGuard says it has identified more than 3,000 AI content farms in connection with its AI content-farm detection work, and its AI Tracking Center frames generative AI as a force multiplier for unreliable news and misinformation operations. ([newsguardtech.com](https://www.newsguardtech.com/press/newsguard-launches-real-time-ai-content-farm-detection-datastream-to-counter-onslaught-of-ai-slop-in-news/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Second, it increases ambient uncertainty. If users cannot tell whether a video, image, article, or voice is synthetic, trust shifts from content-level evaluation to source-level, relational, or cynical shortcuts. A 2025 study of AI-generated misinformation on X Community Notes found AI-generated misinformation was more often entertaining and positive in sentiment, more likely to originate from smaller accounts, and more likely to go viral, though rated slightly less believable and harmful than conventional misinformation. ([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.10266?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Third, AI slop can be optimized for platform aesthetics without human editorial purpose. Kapwing’s 2025 YouTube report estimated that 21–33% of a new user’s feed may consist of AI slop or “brainrot” videos, and identified 278 fully AI-generated channels among 15,000 top global channels. ([kapwing.com](https://www.kapwing.com/blog/ai-slop-report-the-global-rise-of-low-quality-ai-videos/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Fourth, disclosure itself creates a dilemma. 2026 HCI research on AI use in news suggests detailed AI disclosures can reduce trust while increasing source-checking, and users often want “detail-on-demand” rather than either no label or a dense label. ([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.09620?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

## 7. How users learn platform cynicism

Users learn cynicism when they repeatedly experience this sequence:

**The platform recommends something → the thing is low quality → the user engages anyway → more similar things appear → the user concludes the platform is manipulative → the user keeps using it because alternatives are costly or socially absent.**

This is not simple gullibility. It is learned adaptation to opaque systems. Reuters Institute’s 2025 Digital News Report describes traditional news organizations struggling with low trust, declining engagement, stagnating subscriptions, and audience shifts toward video, creators, and platforms. ([reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/digital-news-report/2025/dnr-executive-summary?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) Reuters Institute’s 2026 Digital News Report had not yet been published as of June 11, 2026; it is scheduled for June 16 and is expected to include chapters on AI chatbots, creators, news video, and audience engagement. ([reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk](https://reutersinstitute.politics.ox.ac.uk/news/digital-news-report-2026-will-be-published-tuesday-16-june?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Cynicism has two opposed effects. It can protect users from naive belief, but it can also become nihilism: “everything is fake, everyone is biased, so I might as well click what entertains me or confirms what I feel.” That is fertile ground for junk news, scams, and disinformation-for-profit.

## 8. Vulnerable groups and situated vulnerabilities

“Vulnerability” should not mean stupidity. It usually means a bad match between a person’s needs and a hostile information environment.

| Group/context | Specific vulnerability | Mechanism | Non-victim-blaming response |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Children** | Developing attention, reality testing, ad recognition | Bright repetition, autoplay, character mimicry, parasocial familiarity | Strong defaults, human curation, limits on synthetic kids’ content |
| **Older adults** | Trust-based fraud, isolation, unfamiliar scam formats | Deepfake family/emergency scams, authority impersonation | Intergenerational verification routines, trusted contacts, friction before payment |
| **Low-literacy users** | Higher burden of source evaluation | Dense interfaces, deceptive design, medical/legal jargon | Plain-language trustworthy services, community intermediaries |
| **News avoiders** | Fatigue and helplessness | Negative repetition, low agency | Solutions/agency journalism, reduced alarmism |
| **Highly online users** | Habituation and cynicism | Infinite scroll, social reward loops | Feed controls, friction, self-audit tools |
| **Precarious workers/creators** | Revenue dependence | Algorithmic income volatility | Labor protections, platform transparency, fair compensation |
| **Marginalized communities** | Distrust rooted in real institutional harms | Exploitative narratives can piggyback on justified skepticism | Trustworthy messengers, repair, accountability, not scolding |

Children’s AI slop concerns are now explicit: in April 2026, more than 200 child advocacy organizations and experts urged YouTube and Google to better protect children from low-quality AI-generated videos, including calls for labeling and stronger restrictions on YouTube Kids. ([apnews.com](https://apnews.com/article/68f866c48127222208e1d978b46cbc80?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) Earlier research on YouTube Kids found disturbing or inappropriate videos can mimic children’s content and be recommended from benign starting points, which shows that “slop” problems predate generative AI but are intensified by it. ([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.07046?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

For older adults, the risk is increasingly affective and relational: urgency, fear, and family attachment. FBI reporting for 2025 says Americans over 60 reported about $7.7 billion in losses, up 37% from 2024, and newer HCI work on deepfake scams finds older adults often rely on trusted relationships and intergenerational support to detect impersonation fraud. ([fbi.gov](https://www.fbi.gov/news/press-releases/cryptocurrency-and-ai-scams-bilk-americans-of-billions?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

For low-literacy or health-vulnerable audiences, the problem is not only false belief but information burden. A 2024 JMIR study found many social media users perceive high levels of health misinformation and difficulty discerning it; newer health-literacy work continues to connect low digital health literacy and social-media reliance with susceptibility to health misinformation. ([infodemiology.jmir.org](https://infodemiology.jmir.org/2024/1/e51127?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

## 9. Literacy that helps vs. literacy that blames

Useful literacy teaches users to change their **practice**, not merely to feel smarter. The best-supported practices include lateral reading, source triangulation, emotional self-checks before sharing, click restraint, and recognizing manipulation techniques. Stanford work on lateral reading shows that even short instruction can improve students’ ability to evaluate dubious online sources. ([ed.stanford.edu](https://ed.stanford.edu/news/it-doesn-t-take-long-learn-how-spot-misinformation-online-stanford-study-finds?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) Meta-analytic work suggests media literacy interventions can improve misinformation resilience, but effects vary by design, duration, population, and outcome measure. ([journals.sagepub.com](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/00936502241288103?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Literacy becomes victim-blaming when it says: “Users should simply know better.” That framing ignores interface design, ad-tech incentives, recommender opacity, labor exploitation, political manipulation, and unequal access to time, education, and trusted institutions. Better literacy says: “Here are practices that help, and here are the systems that must change so individual vigilance is not the only defense.”

## 10. Worked examples

### Example A: The curiosity-gap headline

A headline says: “Doctors Are Begging People Over 50 to Stop Eating This One Breakfast Food.” The user suspects it is junk, but the claim is personally relevant, fear-tinged, and incomplete. Clicking resolves the gap. The article turns out to be a thin affiliate page for a supplement or diet product. The user is dissatisfied, but the click, ad impression, and possible retargeting event already happened.

**Mechanism:** curiosity + fear + personal relevance + affiliate monetization.

### Example B: The outrage share

A user sees a misleading post about a public event. They quote-post it with “This is disgusting if true.” They do not fully believe it, but they want to condemn it, warn others, and perform moral alertness. The platform reads this as engagement. Research on outrage and misinformation suggests outrage can increase sharing and that social feedback can teach users to express more outrage later. ([science.org](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.adl2829?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

**Mechanism:** moral emotion + identity signaling + engagement feedback.

### Example C: The AI slop children’s video

A child watches a brightly colored video with familiar cartoon-like characters, repetitive narration, and strange synthetic events. The video is not educational in any meaningful sense, but it holds attention. Autoplay provides another. The parent experiences the content as “empty” or uncanny, but the recommendation system has already learned watch-time signals.

**Mechanism:** sensory salience + repetition + weak curation + watch-time optimization.

### Example D: The local junk-news post

In a local Facebook group, a post claims a school, migrant group, or local official did something alarming. It may be false or AI-generated, but it is locally resonant and socially proximate. Recent UK reporting on Social Market Foundation findings suggests misinformation can be especially prevalent in local “news deserts,” where formal local journalism is weak and social groups become substitute news infrastructures. ([theguardian.com](https://www.theguardian.com/media/2026/jun/08/social-media-groups-fuel-misinfomation-uk-news-deserts-report?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

**Mechanism:** local fear + institutional vacuum + social proximity.

### Example E: The MFA page

A user lands on a “best products” page after search. The page has generic copy, many ads, sticky video, affiliate buttons, and pagination. Even if the user leaves quickly, the page may have triggered multiple ad calls. ANA and related programmatic-advertising work show why such inventory matters: inefficient ad spend and MFA placements are not fringe technicalities; they are structural features of the open-web advertising economy. ([s3.amazonaws.com](https://s3.amazonaws.com/media.mediapost.com/uploads/ANAQ12025Programmatic.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

**Mechanism:** search capture + ad density + affiliate intent + programmatic opacity.

## 11. Risks

1. **Trust collapse:** AI slop and junk news can create a “liar’s dividend,” where authentic evidence is dismissed as fake and fake evidence feels plausible.
2. **Audience exhaustion:** People may avoid news altogether, leaving civic information to influencers, rumor networks, and platform feeds.
3. **Scam intensification:** Synthetic voices, faces, and authority cues make fraud more emotionally convincing.
4. **Labor degradation:** Writers and editors may be displaced into lower-paid cleanup, rewriting, prompt supervision, or content-volume roles.
5. **Child attention capture:** Synthetic children’s media can optimize attention without developmental accountability.
6. **Advertiser waste and brand risk:** MFA inventory can absorb budgets while delivering poor human attention and bad user experiences.
7. **Political manipulation:** Junk news and disinformation-for-profit can convert anger and fear into turnout, donations, harassment, or distrust.
8. **Epistemic inequality:** People with more time, education, and trusted networks can defend themselves better; others face higher verification costs.

## 12. Open research questions

- How often do users knowingly click or share distrusted content, and how should researchers distinguish belief, suspicion, irony, warning, and outrage?
- Which emotions best predict **click-through**, **watch time**, **sharing**, **purchase**, and **belief** separately?
- How does AI slop exposure change baseline trust in authentic journalism, personal images, local information, and expert video?
- What design frictions reduce low-quality engagement without frustrating users or suppressing legitimate speech?
- Can recommender systems optimize for post-consumption satisfaction rather than only immediate engagement?
- How do children learn to recognize synthetic or manipulative media, and at what ages do labels become meaningful?
- Which literacy interventions work for low-literacy, elderly, multilingual, and marginalized communities without shifting responsibility away from platforms?
- How do ad exchanges, affiliate networks, and CMS templates coordinate the economics of junk content?
- What labor protections are needed for writers and editors caught between AI automation, SEO mandates, and platform policy shocks?
- How should researchers study “slop” without turning aesthetic dislike into a proxy for class, race, language, disability, or nonelite media style?
